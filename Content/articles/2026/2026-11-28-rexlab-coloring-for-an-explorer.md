# RexLab: Coloring for an Explorer

[RexLab](https://rexlab.enoa.ai) is one app for a four-year-old and a six-year-old. It does that with age levels: Hatchling for about four, Explorer for about five, Paleontologist for six and up. Each game reads the level and plays its own version of it: more cards in the pair game, a longer maze, more bones in the skeleton.

Coloring had two pictures for each creature, a Hatchling one and an Explorer one, with Paleontologist using the Explorer picture. On paper, that covered every level. In practice, the Explorer pictures had drifted.

## When the cartoon stopped being enough

A Hatchling picture is a friendly cartoon: a big eye, a smile, chubby legs and about 10 to 17 broad regions on a simple ground. That is right for a four-year-old.

The Explorer pictures had slid towards the same style. The first creatures had 37 to 44 regions; later ones had 22 to 27, and looked like a Hatchling picture with a few more shapes. For a five- or six-year-old, that is too simple.

## The standard first, then the drawing

Before any picture was redrawn, the standard was written down in the project's age-level document. An Explorer picture has:

- about 35 to 50 regions;
- the animal in its own proportions, with a small, realistic eye;
- its telling features as regions of their own: plates, armour, a sail and its bands, a frill, a wing membrane, stripes on the flank and tail;
- a scene around it, with sky, landscape and plants.

Two limits keep it playable. Every region stays a broad target a small finger can hit, and small marks such as eyes, teeth and toes are protected ink that a fill never covers. A test guards the 35-region floor. The scenes still waiting for a redraw were listed in that test, so the gap was visible and shrank one picture at a time.

## Scene by scene

Spinosaurus went first: wading a river with a fish leaping at its jaws, its sail, flank and tail banded. T-Rex followed, striding below a smoking volcano with its back and tail stripes, belly, hip and jaw muscle as regions.

Hand-placed shapes only went so far. So the next step was tracing: a generated coloring page with a real illustrator's line, turned by a script into regions under one ink layer. T-Rex was redone that way, then Mosasaurus over a seabed with an ammonite, kelp and light rays; Brachiosaurus with four neck bands by a lake; Parasaurolophus by a swamp river, its crest a region of its own; and Pteranodon gliding over a sea cliff with three panels on each wing. Some pages needed a few closing lines by hand, or a line erased, so that a head or a crest traced as one broad region instead of a sliver.

After the Pteranodon, no Explorer scene was left below the standard, and the waiting list was removed from the test. A child's saved work is never lost on the way: every older picture stays registered, and a saved work reopens on the picture it was made on.

## Fitting the paper

New pictures showed an old layout problem. The paper filled the free space while the 3:2 picture sat letterboxed inside it, leaving wide empty bands, worst on a phone. First the paper became a frame around the picture itself; then the frame went too. Now the picture takes the largest 3:2 size its space allows, with rounded corners, and the traced scenes were retraced whole so that no creature is cut off. A browser test checks this at twelve window sizes.

## Ten new creatures

The same week, ten more creatures joined RexLab, among them Sinosauropteryx, Liopleurodon and Tupandactylus. They came in stages: first the data and texts, hidden; then sizes and facts with their sources; then the illustrations; then the games, from the catalogue to COMPARE, FIND THE PAIR and DIG.

## What the standard bought

Writing the standard first turned "these pictures feel too easy" into a number and a list that a test can check and a drawing can be held to. It did not make the pictures good; the owner still looked at each one. But it said what good meant before anyone drew.
